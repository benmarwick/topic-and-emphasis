Trust in public institutions has declined in many established democracies over the past three decades. Most explanations attribute this decline to economic insecurity, arguing that citizens who feel financially vulnerable withdraw their confidence from governments that fail to protect them. Economic insecurity, however, explains only part of the pattern. In several countries trust fell most sharply among comparatively affluent respondents, whose material circumstances had improved during the same period. We argue that these respondents reacted less to their own situation than to visible failures of administrative competence. Using panel data from four national surveys, we show that perceived competence predicts subsequent changes in trust more reliably than household income does.
