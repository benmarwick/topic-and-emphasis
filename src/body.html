A revision workbench for research writing, built on Gopen and Swan’s “The Science of Scientific Writing” (1990).

About this workbench

Gopen and Swan showed that readers look for particular things in particular places: the verb soon after its subject, familiar material at the opening of a sentence, and the news at its close. Paste a draft and the lenses mark where its structure meets those expectations and where it works against them. Tap a sentence to rewrite it in place and watch the marks change.

New here? Try the , or on six short sentences, before you paste your own draft. Or let the page .

You do not need to settle every note. Each note is a question about what a reader expects, and a sentence can be right as it stands. Mark it as checked and move on. George Orwell closed his own six rules for writers by telling them to break any of the six before writing something ugly. They are in “Politics and the English Language” (1946).

      Topic & Emphasis

      What this is

      A revision workbench for research writing. It marks a draft with questions about what a reader expects to find, and where, so that the writer can revise it. It does not rewrite anything. The method is that of George Gopen and Judith Swan’s “The Science of Scientific Writing” (1990), with later work on cohesion, vocabulary, hedging and argument added lens by lens.

      Author

      Ben Marwick chose the sources, set the design and its constraints, and is responsible for the result. The code was written with the help of an AI coding tool (Claude, by Anthropic) under his direction.

      How to cite

      For the curious
      
        
      
        
        
          
      • Rules, not a language model. Every mark and every note comes from a fixed rule, so the same text always gets the same marks. The rules read structure and know nothing of meaning. They have no parser: sentences are split by punctuation, and verbs and subjects are found by word lists and position, so some sentences are misjudged.
        • 
          
      • Thresholds. Where a published norm exists it is cited. Where the page uses a working threshold of its own, the Guide says so.
        • 
          
      • What the optional parts may do. The meaning model and the optional data add marks, counts and pictures. They never make a note and never change the record of your revision.
        • 
          
      • Checked against judgement. The Guide reports how each count tracks teachers’ ratings of reading ease on 4,721 passages. Several track them weakly, and the Guide says which.
        • 
          
      • Tests. Eighteen test files run the rule engine against fixed sentences before each release. The pictures are checked by eye, on constructed drafts more than on real papers.
        • 
          
      • What is kept, and where. Your draft, the draft you began with, your choices and your settings are kept in this browser’s local storage, on this device, so that work survives a closed tab. Nothing is sent to a server. To erase the draft, use “Erase the saved draft” under “About this tool” at the foot of the page. To erase the settings as well, clear this site’s data in the browser’s settings.
        • 
          
      • What can leave the page. Only a phrase you choose to look up, eight words at most, sent to the site you pick. The typefaces and the optional files are fetched; the draft is not part of any request.
        • 
          
      • For other programs. The repository includes a Model Context Protocol server that gives an AI agent the same rules, so that a tutor built on one cannot disagree with this page.
        • 
          
      • How it is made. One HTML file, built by a short Python script from four source files. The pictures are SVG drawn by hand-written code.

      About this tool
      
      
      Deterministic, with no AI unless you add it. Every mark and every note comes from a fixed rule written into this page, so the same text always produces the same marks. No language model or other AI is involved, here or on any server, unless you switch on the optional meaning model described below. That model never makes a note.
      
      
      Private, and usable offline. Your text is analysed inside this browser tab. It is never uploaded or stored on any server. Once the page has loaded the rules need no internet connection. The page fetches its typefaces, and falls back to system fonts without them. Everything else it fetches is optional and comes from this site only when you ask: the word norms, the Academic Word List, the Academic Formulas List, the phrase bank and the meaning model. None of those requests carries your text. Two things do reach other sites, and only when you choose them. A link in a note or in the Guide opens another site in a new tab and sends it nothing from your draft. The phrase look-up under the rewrite box sends the words you select, eight at most, to SkELL or Linggle. So that you do not lose work, this browser keeps your draft on this device and nowhere else. 
      
      
      Phrases. Some notes link to a section of the University of Manchester’s Academic Phrasebank and to the matching page of UEFAP’s rhetorical functions (Gillett). Those links open in a new tab and need a connection, and none of the text of either site is copied here. The sentence openers, wording ladders and move checklists inside this tool are its own. The Phrases tab under the rewrite box is different: it holds about 590 sentence frames from Wallwork (2016), as prepared in the open-source package academic-phrases with the permission of the publisher and the author. They are optional data under the GNU GPL 3, loaded only when you ask.
      
      
      Statistics. The p values are recomputed with standard formulas for t, F, r, chi-square and z tests, written for this page, and only for results reported in the usual form, such as t(28) = 2.20, p = .036. A result written another way is not checked, so no mark does not mean no error.
      
      
      Word lists. The Vocabulary level lens uses the New General Service List and the New Academic Word List by Charles Browne, Brent Culligan and Joseph Phillips. Both are built into this page under a Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 licence, so the copies here may be reused on the same terms. The Academic Word List (Coxhead 2000) is optional: its 570 headwords, with their families and sublists, are fetched when you press “Load the Academic Word List” in that lens, and you can paste a copy of your own. A word on none of these lists is not an error. The lists cover general English, so every field has ordinary terms that fall outside them.
      
      
      Length and rhythm. The four length bands are fixed, so a sentence gets the same colour in any draft. Their edges sit on the scale of average sentence lengths that Rudolf Flesch published in 1948. The rules for runs, for words before the main verb and for neighbour contrast are this tool’s working values, set out with their sources in the guide inside that lens. They are not published norms, and the lens sets no target score.
      
      
      Meaning model (optional). Everything on this page is rule-based unless you switch on the meaning model. That is a small sentence-embedding model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) which turns each sentence into numbers so that sentences can be compared by meaning. It is a trained language model, and so a form of AI. It runs entirely in this browser, generates no text, sends nothing anywhere, and gives the same result for the same text. Its results appear only in the boxes and lines labelled as coming from the meaning model, in ten lenses (Subject → verb, Topic grid, Old → new, Reverse outline, Claim tracer, Source overlap, Term tracker, Clutter, Before & after and the Load map), in the Meaning views lens, and in two gauges under the rewrite box. The notes, the counts of open notes and the revision receipt are never affected by it. It needs the model files that sit beside the page in its GitHub repository, so it is not available in a copy of the page saved on its own.
      
      
      Research measures. The Research profile and the box headed “Research measure” in most of the lenses reproduce counts described in published research on cohesion, vocabulary, syntax, readability and mechanical errors. The publications are named on each box and listed under Sources. Every count is recomputed here by a fixed rule. This page has no parser, no part-of-speech tagger, no lemmatiser and no model of meaning, so its figures will not match the published tools exactly; each box says how its count differs. The figures describe a draft. None of them is a score, and none has a target.
      
      
      More word lists, and optional data. Ten more lists are built into the page: the Academic Keyword List (Paquot 2010), which its publisher, the Centre for English Corpus Linguistics at UCLouvain, licenses under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0; four lists compiled by Titus Wormer under the MIT licence (hedges, weasel words, fillers and buzzwords), which are counted in Hedging, Vague & worn words and Clutter and raise no notes; the familiar words of the New Dale–Chall formula, the style words from the excess-vocabulary list of Kobak et al. (2025), the general science jargon of Plavén-Sigray et al. (2017), syllable counts for 1,961 common words from the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary, and the promotional adjectives reported by Millar, Batalo & Budgell (2022). The rest is optional and is fetched only when you ask. A part-of-speech lexicon of about 54,000 words (the lexicon of the Brill tagger, MIT licence) loads from Subject → verb or Dense phrases, and gives a second opinion on the main verb and a count of nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs. The Academic Formulas List (Simpson-Vlach & Ellis 2010), with 607 word sequences and the formula teaching worth of each, loads from Signal phrases. It and the Academic Word List are stored as words and numbers only, credited to their authors, and no permission was sought for either; the repository’s data folder sets out the position taken. Like the phrase bank, they are looked up by rule, they mark and count, and they raise no notes. Four published sets of word ratings (concreteness, age of acquisition, frequency and prevalence) are optional: they are fetched only when you press “Load word norms” in Vocabulary level, they are looked up by rule, and they raise no notes. Those four files keep their authors’ licences, which allow non-commercial use and are listed in the repository’s data folder.
      
      
      Limits. The rules read structure and have no idea what your sentences mean. They will misjudge some sentences, especially where a verb form doubles as a noun or modifier. When a rule is wrong, choose “I checked this” and move on.
