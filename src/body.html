Topic&Emphasis

A revision workbench for research writing, built on Gopen and Swan’s “The Science of Scientific Writing” (1990).

About this workbench

Gopen and Swan showed that readers look for particular things in particular places: the verb soon after its subject, familiar material at the opening of a sentence, and the news at its close. Paste a draft and the lenses mark where its structure meets those expectations and where it works against them. Tap a sentence to rewrite it in place and watch the marks change.

New here? Try the , or on six short sentences, before you paste your own draft. Or let the page .

You do not need to settle every note. Each note is a question about what a reader expects, and a sentence can be right as it stands. Mark it as checked and move on. George Orwell closed his own six rules for writers by telling them to break any of the six before writing something ugly. They are in “Politics and the English Language” (1946).

      About this tool

      Deterministic, with no AI. Every mark comes from a fixed rule written into this page, so the same text always produces the same marks. No language model or other AI is involved, here or on any server.

      Private, and usable offline. Your text is analysed inside this browser tab. It is never uploaded, sent or stored on any server. Once the page has loaded the analysis needs no internet connection; the only thing the page fetches is its typefaces, and it falls back to system fonts without them. So that you do not lose work, this browser keeps your draft on this device and nowhere else.

      Phrasebank links. Some notes link to a section of the University of Manchester’s Academic Phrasebank. Those links open its site in a new tab and need a connection. The sentence openers, wording ladders and move checklists inside this tool are its own, and none of the Phrasebank’s text is copied here.

      Statistics. The p values are recomputed with standard formulas for t, F, r, chi-square and z tests, written for this page, and only for results reported in the usual form, such as t(28) = 2.20, p = .036. A result written another way is not checked, so no mark does not mean no error.

      Word lists. The Vocabulary level lens uses the New General Service List and the New Academic Word List by Charles Browne, Brent Culligan and Joseph Phillips. Both are built into this page under a Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 licence, so the copies here may be reused on the same terms. A word on neither list is not an error. The lists cover general English, so every field has ordinary terms that fall outside them.

      Length and rhythm. The four length bands are fixed, so a sentence gets the same colour in any draft. Their edges sit on the scale of average sentence lengths that Rudolf Flesch published in 1948. The rules for runs, for words before the main verb and for neighbour contrast are this tool’s working values, set out with their sources in the guide inside that lens. They are not published norms, and the lens sets no target score.

      Meaning model (optional). Everything on this page is rule-based unless you switch on the meaning model. That is a small sentence-embedding model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) which turns each sentence into numbers so that sentences can be compared by meaning. It is a trained language model, and so a form of AI. It runs entirely in this browser, generates no text, sends nothing anywhere, and gives the same result for the same text. Its results appear only in the boxes and lines labelled as coming from the meaning model, in six lenses: Topic grid, Reverse outline, Claim tracer, Source overlap, Term tracker and Before & after. The notes, the counts of open notes and the revision receipt are never affected by it. It needs the model files that sit beside the page in its GitHub repository, so it is not available in a copy of the page saved on its own.

      Research measures. The Research profile and the box headed “Research measure” in fourteen of the lenses reproduce counts described in published research on cohesion, vocabulary, syntax, readability and mechanical errors. The publications are named on each box and listed under Sources. Every count is recomputed here by a fixed rule. This page has no parser, no part-of-speech tagger, no lemmatiser and no model of meaning, so its figures will not match the published tools exactly; each box says how its count differs. The figures describe a draft. None of them is a score, and none has a target.

      Limits. The rules read structure and have no idea what your sentences mean. They will misjudge some sentences, especially where a verb form doubles as a noun or modifier. When a rule is wrong, choose “I checked this” and move on.
